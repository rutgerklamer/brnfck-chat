Culture

Müller made brainfuck to get a small compiler and “moved on to other things”, in his words. The language “had served its purpose. Or so I thought” [1]. What happened next was done by other people, for their own reasons: the pleasure of a hard puzzle with simple rules, the sport of making programs as short as possible, and the wish to understand how computers work underneath.

Why people do this

A puzzle with a guaranteed answer

The README of 1993 ends its description with a dare: "Who can program anything useful with it? :)" [2] Because the language can compute anything computable (see Theory), every task has a solution. Finding it is the game. Mats Linander ends a long technical article on the subject with the plainest statement of the motive we found: "mucking around with brainfuck is just plain fun" [3].

Constraints

Taking nearly everything away makes ordinary things interesting again. Adding two numbers is a small invention. Printing a number in decimal is a real program. People who like sonnets or chess problems will know the feeling.

A way of thinking about computing

Michael Mateas and Nick Montfort, who studied such "weird languages" as an art form, put it this way: minimal languages "comment on the space of computation". They draw attention to how little structure a universal machine needs [4]. For them brainfuck is the model case: "an archetypically minimalist language".

Learning

The language is small enough to implement in an evening and real enough to be worth it. Müller: "you can write that one in half an hour" [1]. So it is a common first project for people learning how interpreters and compilers work. Eli Bendersky built a whole series on just-in-time compilation around it [5], and a brainfuck interpreter is one of the standard tasks in a widely used comparison of programming languages [6].

The author looks at all this with amused surprise. On an implementation of brainfuck inside the game Minecraft: "People are weird." On the programs: "these people scare me" [1].

Code golf: as short as possible

Code golf is the sport of solving a task in the fewest characters. Brainfuck is a natural course for it: programs are long by nature, so every saving counts, and the tricks are strange and deep.

The best-documented case is "Hello, World!". On the Code Golf site of Stack Exchange, the user primo posted a program of 78 commands in January 2016 and offered a standing reward to anyone who could beat it. In April 2018 the user KSab answered with 72, found by a search program written for the purpose [7]. Details are under Notable programs.

Other standing records:

  • Numbers. The Esolang wiki keeps the shortest known program for every number from 0 to 255, with and without wrapping [8].
  • The interpreter in itself. Cristofani's dbfi, 423 commands, described in a paper as possibly the shortest of its kind [9] [10].
  • Implementations. The smallest compiler and interpreter in machine code, a contest as old as the language. See The smallest.

Contests

Organised contests have been held since at least 1999, when the Hugi size-coding competition set the task of the smallest interpreter and received entries of 98, 100 and 102 bytes [11].

The community held its own. brainfuck.org keeps the results of several "Brainfuck Golf" contests (shortest program for a task such as sorting bytes), of a "Brainfuck Component Competition" and of a "Logical Brainfuck Competition", and photographs of a prize: an abacus with an inscription [12]. The Brainfuck Archive has a folder of golf results too [13].

A game: BF Joust

BF Joust turns the language into a fight. Two programs run at the same time on one tape of 10 to 30 cells, starting at opposite ends. Each guards a "flag" in its own first cell and wins by bringing the other's flag to zero or by making the other leave the tape. A match is played over every tape length. The game was invented by Kerim Aydin in a community that plays a game of changing rules, and it has a permanent tournament ("hill") run by a chat robot, to which anyone can send a program [14].

Obfuscation, and code as speech

A program in brainfuck is hard to read, and that is an attraction too. It stands in the tradition of obfuscated programming, which Mateas and Montfort discuss alongside weird languages [4].

Once it served an argument. David Touretzky of Carnegie Mellon University collected versions of a legally disputed program in as many forms as he could find, among them a version in brainfuck, to show that program code cannot be cleanly separated from other kinds of expression. The gallery describes the brainfuck exhibit as "a truly minimalist description of the algorithm" [15].

And there is a T-shirt. Müller wore one to his talk with a short brainfuck program on it. Run it and it prints "GEEK" [1].

Limits

  • The reasons under “Why people do this” are drawn from what people say about their own work. Nobody has surveyed brainfuck programmers.
  • Communities move. The places in the table were there on 11 October 2026. We list only places whose pages we opened; there are active groups elsewhere that we did not look at.
  • We took the rules of BF Joust from the wiki and did not play it.
