History

In 1993 Urban Müller looked after a large archive of Amiga software. Someone uploaded a language called FALSE whose compiler was only 1,024 bytes long. Müller wanted a smaller one, and got it by making the language simpler [1]. The package he published in June 1993 holds a compiler of 240 bytes [2]. The language turned out to be nearly the same as one that the logician Corrado Böhm had described in 1964 [3].

1993: a compiler of 1,024 bytes, and a smaller one

The author tells it himself in a talk he gave in 2017 [1]. In 1993 he was moderating what he calls the largest Amiga software archive. This was Aminet, which he had founded the year before [4]. One day someone uploaded a programming language called FALSE. Its compiler was 1,024 bytes long, which he found "ridiculously small".

FALSE was the work of Wouter van Oortmerssen, who designed it in 1993 with a compiler of "just 1024 bytes", written in 68000 assembler, the machine language of the Amiga's processor [5]. It is a language with a stack, variables, functions without names, and conditions and loops [6].

His first reaction: "Can we do better?" [1] Improving on the size of the compiler for that language would have been hard. He chose another way: "if the program doesn't fit the problem, change the problem" [1]. He took things out of the language until almost nothing was left. Numbers written out became "add one, many times". Variables with names became cells on a tape. FALSE's loop became a plain "while" [1]. What remained were eight commands.

A second witness. Van Oortmerssen's page on FALSE links to Müller's talk and says it explains how seeing FALSE led to brainfuck [5].

The original package

The package is still where it was put: the file brainfuck-2.lha on Aminet, dated 9 June 1993 [2]. It is 10 kilobytes and holds eleven files:

README
2,863 bytes: the language, the compiler, the interpreter
bfc
the compiler, 240 bytes
bfc.asm
its source, in 68000 assembler
bfi, bfi.c
an interpreter "for debugging" and its source in C, 1,047 bytes of text
six programs
hello.b, prime.b, atoi.b, div10.b, mul10.b, varia.b

The "2" in the name is a version number. Under the heading "WHATS NEW" the README says [2]:

Three facts follow from this passage.

  • There was an earlier version, of 296 bytes. 240 plus 56 is 296. That is the figure Wikipedia and a recent book give for the first compiler [7] [8]. We found no copy of that first version: Aminet lists only this second one and a port to another language uploaded in 2022.
  • The first version had cells of 32 bits. A "longword" on the Amiga is 32 bits. The familiar cell of one byte came with the second version, as a way to save space in the compiler.
  • The small size had a price. Every compiled program is 65,536 bytes long, whatever it does, and needs version 2.0 of the Amiga's operating system.

The README thanks Chris Schneider "for his ideas how to make the compiler shorter", and the short description on Aminet ends with a question that people have been answering ever since: "Who can program anything useful with it? :)" [2]

When exactly?

The oldest files in the package, two of the example programs, are dated 21 May 1993, and the package itself 9 June 1993 [2]. So the language existed in May 1993 at the latest, and the first version came out before 9 June. Wikipedia's summary box gives "September 1993" as the first appearance [7]. The archive does not support that date.

Later sizes

Müller says in his talk that the compiler was "later reduced to 200" bytes [1], and Brian Raiter's page says he got it under 200 [9]. We did not find such a version to check.

The name

The name came last, when the compiler was done. Müller says he was trying, and failing, to express uselessness and confusion, and that it "turned out to be a better name than I had originally intended" [1]. He wrote it in lower case throughout the README [2], and that is still the convention, though the name is now often written with a capital [10]. Because of the swear word, many texts write it another way: see the questions page.

Ancestors

P′′, 1964

Twenty-nine years earlier the Italian computer scientist Corrado Böhm had described a language he called P′′ ("P double prime"), to study a family of Turing machines [3]. It has a tape of cells, one command that moves right, one that adds one to a cell and moves left, and a pair of brackets that repeat a piece of program while the current cell is not zero. Böhm showed that these are enough to compute anything that can be computed [3].

Brainfuck without its input and output commands is almost the same thing. The Esolang wiki calls brainfuck "a minor variation" of P′′ and notes that Böhm's paper already used six symbols that match + - < > [ ] [10]. Frans Faase, who keeps a set of pages about the language, also points out that the result was known in 1964 [11].

Did Müller know P′′? The Esolang wiki says it is not known [10]. In his own account he does not mention it: he describes getting there by cutting down FALSE [1]. That is all we can say.

The structured program theorem. In 1966 Böhm and Giuseppe Jacopini published the result that made the idea famous: sequence, choice and repetition are enough to write any program, with no jumps [3]. Brainfuck is a small living proof.

The Turing machine, 1936

Both languages descend from the machine that Alan Turing described in 1936 to define what "computing" means: a tape, a head that reads and writes one square, and a table of rules [12]. Brainfuck's tape and pointer are that tape and that head. The page on theory explains the connection.

Strange languages before 1993

Languages made as a joke or an experiment were not new. The earliest known one made deliberately so is INTERCAL, designed in 1972 by Donald Woods and James Lyon [13]. But 1993 was a remarkable year: FALSE, brainfuck and Befunge all appeared. Befunge, by Chris Pressey, lays its program out on a grid and was made to be as hard to compile as possible [14], the opposite of Müller's aim.

How it spread

The language left the Amiga within a few years. A program that reads eight symbols is quick to write on any machine, and the package held an interpreter in portable C that anyone could compile [2]. What follows is what we can date.

  • Small implementations. In 1999 Brian Raiter published a compiler for Linux of 166 bytes [15] [9], and the Hugi size-coding competition asked for the smallest interpreter; the winning program for DOS had 98 bytes [16] [17].
  • Personal pages. Raiter, Frans Faase and Daniel Cristofani each kept pages with descriptions, programs and proofs [9] [11] [18]. Cristofani's became brainfuck.org.
  • An archive. Jeff Johnston collected programs and tools in the Brainfuck Archive, later kept by Panu Kalliokoski; most of its files date from 2002 to 2005 [19].
  • A community. A chat channel for esoteric languages has existed since December 2002 [20]. The Esolang wiki became the place where conventions and results are written down [10].
  • Ambitious programs. In 2003 came Erik Bosman's Mandelbrot viewer [21], a compiler from BASIC [22] and a paper on a very short interpreter written in brainfuck itself [23]. In 2005 Jon Ripley published a text adventure of two megabytes [24].
  • Outside notice. In 2005 a paper on the aesthetics of code called brainfuck "an archetypically minimalist language" [25]. In 2022 a book on unusual languages gave it a chapter [8]. In 2024 a research group used a variant of it to study how self-copying programs can arise from random ones [26].

Why this one?

The Esolang wiki says that the most popular esoteric languages are "probably Brainfuck and Befunge", and that many newer ones are heavily influenced by the two [13]. Its author found 700,000 pages on Google that mention it in 2017 and was surprised: he had "semi-expected" that people would write compilers and interpreters, "because it's very easy", but not the rest [1]. Nobody has measured why. These reasons seem likely to us; they are an interpretation, not a finding.

  • You can hold all of it in your head. Eight commands, one table. You know the language after five minutes.
  • You can implement it in an evening. The original interpreter is about a thousand characters of C [2]. So it became a standard first exercise, and a standard test for comparing languages [27] and for teaching how interpreters and compilers work [28].
  • It is a real language. Anything computable can be written in it (see Theory). That turns every program into a puzzle with a guaranteed solution.
  • The name is hard to forget.
  • It is easy to vary. Change the symbols or add a command and you have a new language. Many people did (see Relatives).

What we could not verify

  • The first version. We know it existed and that its compiler was 56 bytes larger, from the README of the second [2]. We did not find the file or its date.
  • The day the language was made. The oldest dated file is from 21 May 1993. Anything more exact is a guess.
  • "September 1993". Wikipedia's date for the first appearance [7] is later than the package on Aminet. We could not find where it comes from.
  • A compiler under 200 bytes. Stated by Müller and by Raiter [1] [9]; we did not find the program.
  • Whether Müller knew Böhm's P′′. Unknown [10]. His own account does not mention it [1].
  • The talk itself. We read a community transcript, which says its last page is missing [1]. We did not watch the recording.
  • Böhm's paper of 1964. We rely on descriptions of it [3] [10] and did not read the paper.
  • Why it became the best known. The reasons given under “Why this one?” are our interpretation.
