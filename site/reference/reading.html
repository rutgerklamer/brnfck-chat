Start here

Six sources that together give you the language, its story, how to write it and how to run it fast. All are short.

  1. Urban Müller, brainfuck-2.lha, Aminet, dev/lang, dated 9 June 1993: the package page on Aminet. We read the files in the archive: README, bfc.asm (the compiler), bfi.c (the interpreter) and the six example programs. Sizes and dates are those of the archive listing.

    Why read it: The original distribution, and the nearest thing the language has to a definition. The README is 2,863 bytes and takes two minutes to read. Read it before anything else.

  2. Urban Müller, talk at Tamedia TX, 2017, read as the community transcript on the Esolang wiki: Tamedia TX 2017 transcript. The transcript says its last page is missing. Wikipedia gives the title as "Brainfuck, or how I learned to change the problem" and the date as 13 June 2017; we did not watch the recording.

    Why read it: The author telling the story himself, 24 years later. Short and funny. The only source we found for his motives in his own words.

  3. Daniel B Cristofani, "Yet another brainfuck reference": the reference on brainfuck.org.

    Why read it: One careful page by the language's best-known programmer. Says exactly what is defined and what is "unpredictable".

  4. Esolang wiki, "Brainfuck": the wiki article on brainfuck. A community wiki; we use it for conventions and for its list of results, and name it where it is our only source.

    Why read it: The best single page on the language as it is used today: conventions, examples, the computational class, and many links.

  5. Daniel B Cristofani, "get good at brainfuck": the series on brainfuck.org.

    Why read it: A series that walks through his programs in the order he wrote them. The author calls it unfinished. The best next step after Brainfuck basics.

  6. Mats Linander, "brainfuck optimization strategies", 7 January 2015: the article on calmerthanyouare.org.

    Why read it: The clearest account of the standard optimisations, each measured on six real programs. Start here if you want to write a fast implementation.

Descriptions of the language

Careful accounts of what is defined, what is not, and what implementations should do.

  • Esolang wiki, "Brainfuck": the wiki article on brainfuck. A community wiki; we use it for conventions and for its list of results, and name it where it is our only source.

    Why read it: The best single page on the language as it is used today: conventions, examples, the computational class, and many links.

  • Daniel B Cristofani, "Yet another brainfuck reference": the reference on brainfuck.org.

    Why read it: One careful page by the language's best-known programmer. Says exactly what is defined and what is "unpredictable".

  • Daniel B Cristofani, "The Epistle to the Implementors": the epistle on brainfuck.org.

    Why read it: Advice to people who write interpreters: newlines, end of input, the # and ! conventions. Read it before you write one.

  • Brian Raiter, "Portable Brainfuck": the portability page on muppetlabs.com.

    Why read it: An informal standard: the least a program may count on.

  • Brian Raiter, "Brainfuck: An Eight-Instruction Turing-Complete Programming Language": the brainfuck page on muppetlabs.com.

    Why read it: One of the oldest brainfuck pages still online, with a 166-byte compiler for Linux, a quine and a factoring program.

  • Daniel B Cristofani, tests.b: test programs for implementations on brainfuck.org.

    Why read it: Small programs that tell you how an implementation treats newlines, end of input and the edges of the tape.

Theory

Proofs, and the older work the language connects to.

  • Wikipedia, "P′′": the Wikipedia article on P′′. Our source for Corrado Böhm, "On a family of Turing machines and the related programming language", ICC Bulletin 3 (1964), 185 to 194, and for C. Böhm and G. Jacopini, "Flow Diagrams, Turing Machines and Languages with Only Two Formation Rules", Communications of the ACM 9(5) (1966), 366 to 371. We did not open the two papers themselves.

    Why read it: The language of 1964 that brainfuck closely resembles. The article gives the instruction set and the exact correspondence.

  • Frans Faase, "BF is Turing-complete": the proof page on iwriteiam.nl.

    Why read it: Three routes to the result on one page: translating a Turing machine, Cristofani's universal machine, and register machines.

  • Frans Faase, "Brainf***": the brainfuck pages on iwriteiam.nl.

    Why read it: An early enthusiast's collection: an introduction, proofs, an interpreter written in brainfuck, and links.

  • Daniel B Cristofani, utm.b: the universal Turing machine on brainfuck.org. Its comments name the machine it implements: Yurii Rogozhin, "Small universal Turing machines", Theoretical Computer Science 168(2) (1996), 215 to 240, which we did not open.

    Why read it: A universal Turing machine as a commented brainfuck program. The comments are a lesson in themselves.

  • Alan J. Perlis, "Epigrams on Programming", ACM SIGPLAN Notices 17(9), September 1982, read in the copy at Yale: the epigrams at cs.yale.edu.

    Why read it: The source of "Turing tar-pit" (epigram 54). A list of one-liners, many of them still quoted.

Writing and generating programs

Cookbooks, and tools that write brainfuck.

  • Esolang wiki, "Brainfuck algorithms": the wiki's collection of algorithms.

    Why read it: The standard cookbook: assignment, arithmetic, comparison, if and else, arrays, printing numbers. Each recipe says whether it needs wrapping cells.

  • Esolang wiki, "Brainfuck constants": the wiki's table of constants.

    Why read it: The shortest known way to make each number from 0 to 255.

  • Esolang wiki, "Brainfuck code generation": the wiki's list of tools that write brainfuck.

    Why read it: Macro systems and compilers that have brainfuck as their output.

  • Esolang wiki, "BFBASIC": the wiki article on BFBASIC.

    Why read it: Jeffry Johnston's compiler from BASIC to brainfuck (2003).

  • Esolang wiki, "C2BF": the wiki article on C2BF.

    Why read it: Gregor Richards's partial C compiler, with a description of how it lays out a stack and a heap on the tape.

  • shinh, ELVM Compiler Infrastructure, README: shinh/elvm on GitHub.

    Why read it: Compiles C to an intermediate form and from there to brainfuck and some sixty other targets.

Programs

The notable ones, at their authors' own addresses.

  • Daniel B Cristofani, brainfuck.org: the main page. The page licenses his programs under Creative Commons Attribution-ShareAlike 4.0.

    Why read it: The best collection of hand-written programs there is, most with comments: sorting, Life, tic-tac-toe, e, a universal Turing machine, and contest results.

  • Daniel B Cristofani, "get good at brainfuck": the series on brainfuck.org.

    Why read it: A series that walks through his programs in the order he wrote them. The author calls it unfinished. The best next step after Brainfuck basics.

  • Daniel B Cristofani, dbfi.b: the self-interpreter on brainfuck.org. The count of 423 commands is ours, of the file without its closing comment.

    Why read it: A brainfuck interpreter in a few lines of brainfuck.

  • Oleg Mazonka and Daniel B. Cristofani, "A Very Short Self-Interpreter", arXiv:cs/0311032, 21 November 2003: the abstract on arXiv. We read the abstract only.

    Why read it: The paper about dbfi.

  • Frans Faase, "BF interpreter written in BF": the interpreter page on iwriteiam.nl.

    Why read it: An earlier self-interpreter, made by a C program that writes brainfuck.

  • Erik Bosman, Mandelbrot set viewer, files dated 1 June 2003, in the Brainfuck Archive: the mandelbrot directory with its README.txt.

    Why read it: The famous Mandelbrot program with the macro files it was made from.

  • Code Golf Stack Exchange, answers to the "Hello, World!" challenge: the answer by KSab (27 April 2018, 72 bytes) and the answer by primo (3 January 2016, 78 bytes). Read through the Stack Exchange API.

    Why read it: The record-holding short programs, with their authors' explanations of how they work and how they were found.

  • Jon Ripley, "The Lost Kingdom Brainfuck Edition", 2004 to 2005: the game's page on jonripley.com.

    Why read it: A text adventure of two megabytes, and its author's notes.

  • Linus Åkesson, "Game of Life in Brainfuck": the page on linusakesson.net.

    Why read it: Conway's Life, written by hand, with the author's answers to readers.

  • mitxela, bf-tic-tac-toe, 2017: mitxela/bf-tic-tac-toe on GitHub.

    Why read it: Tic-tac-toe against the computer, with the commented source.

  • "Writing a ray tracer in Brainfuck", 24 September 2026: the article on epestr.com. All figures are the author's own.

    Why read it: A recent account of generating a 23 MB program from a small language of the author's own.

  • Ad1th, lobotoml: Ad1th/lobotoml on GitHub. All figures are from its README.

    Why read it: A neural network of 7 neurons (2, 4 and 1) for XOR, compiled to a brainfuck program of 9,731 bytes.

  • Blaise Agüera y Arcas and others, "Computational Life: How Well-formed, Self-replicating Programs Emerge from Simple Interaction", arXiv:2406.19108, 2024: the abstract on arXiv and the full text.

    Why read it: Research that uses a brainfuck variant as the "physics" of an artificial world in which self-copying programs appear.

  • This site's own project. Counts and measurements are on How it works: the numbers and How do I know it is real?; the earlier work we know of is under Has this been done before?.

    Why read it: Three chat models as brainfuck programs, with the checks that were done and the limits.

Implementations and how to make them fast

Explanations, measurements and well-known implementations.

  • Mats Linander, "brainfuck optimization strategies", 7 January 2015: the article on calmerthanyouare.org.

    Why read it: The clearest account of the standard optimisations, each measured on six real programs. Start here if you want to write a fast implementation.

  • Eli Bendersky, "Adventures in JIT compilation: Part 1, an interpreter", 20 March 2017: part 1 on eli.thegreenplace.net.

    Why read it: Four interpreters, each faster than the last, with timings. Uses brainfuck to teach how interpreters work.

  • Eli Bendersky, "Adventures in JIT compilation: Part 2, an x64 JIT", 2017: part 2 on eli.thegreenplace.net.

    Why read it: The same programs translated to machine code while they run.

  • Wilfred Hughes, bfc: the bfc site, its page "Optimisations" and the repository.

    Why read it: "An industrial grade compiler" for brainfuck, with its optimisations documented one by one, with examples.

  • rdebath, Brainfuck repository: rdebath/Brainfuck on GitHub. Holds the Tritium interpreter, translators to many languages, test programs and a copy of Müller's 1993 compiler.

    Why read it: A large toolbox: a very fast interpreter, tests such as bitwidth.b, and translators from brainfuck to dozens of languages.

  • Kang Seonghoon, Esotope Brainfuck Compiler, README: lifthrasiir/esotope-bfc on GitHub.

    Why read it: The compiler that turns a "Hello World!" program into one print statement.

  • Mats Linander, awib, README dated 14 September 2014: matslina/awib on GitHub.

    Why read it: A brainfuck compiler written in brainfuck, with several back ends.

  • Brian Raiter, bf.asm, copyright 1999: the assembly source on muppetlabs.com.

    Why read it: A whole compiler in 166 bytes of Linux executable, with comments.

  • Esolang wiki, "Brainfuck implementations": the wiki's list of implementations.

    Why read it: A long list: interpreters, compilers, hardware. Uneven, but the widest net.

  • Hugi Size Coding Competition, list of past competitions: the competition archive on hugi.scene.org. Competition 6 (1999) asked for a brainfuck interpreter.

    Why read it: The results of a 1999 contest for the smallest interpreter.

  • Surma, bfwasm: surma/bfwasm on GitHub.

    Why read it: A small compiler from brainfuck to WebAssembly that says of itself that it does not optimise.

  • mrjameshamilton, bf: mrjameshamilton/bf on GitHub.

    Why read it: One optimising front end, many back ends: JVM, C, LLVM IR, ARM, WebAssembly, JavaScript.

  • kostya, benchmarks: kostya/benchmarks on GitHub.

    Why read it: A well-known comparison of programming languages. One of its tests is a brainfuck interpreter.

Culture and scholarship

Writing about why, and the places where the community is.

  • Esolang wiki, "Esoteric programming language": the wiki article on esoteric languages.

    Why read it: What an esoteric language is, a short history from INTERCAL (1972), and the kinds there are.

  • David S. Touretzky, "Gallery of CSS Descramblers", Carnegie Mellon University: the gallery at cs.cmu.edu.

    Why read it: A collection made to argue that program code is speech. One exhibit is a descrambler in brainfuck.

  • Michael Mateas and Nick Montfort, "A Box, Darkly: Obfuscation, Weird Languages, and Code Aesthetics", Proceedings of the 6th Digital Arts and Culture Conference, IT University of Copenhagen, 1 to 3 December 2005, pages 144 to 153: the paper on nickm.com (PDF).

    Why read it: A scholarly look at why people write obfuscated code and invent strange languages. Has a section on brainfuck as minimalism.

  • Ronald T. Kneusel, Strange Code, No Starch Press, 2022, chapter 10, read as the publisher's sample: chapter 10 on nostarch.com (PDF).

    Why read it: A book chapter that teaches the language (as "BF") and walks through Müller's original interpreter.

  • Esolang wiki, "Community portal": the wiki's community portal.

    Why read it: Where the people are: the chat channel, its logs, forums.

  • The Brainfuck Archive, maintained by Panu Kalliokoski, first collected by Jeff Johnston: the archive at esoteric.sange.fi and its README.txt.

    Why read it: An old file archive of programs, implementations and tools. Most files date from 2002 to 2005.

For orientation only

Encyclopedia articles. Good maps, not evidence.

  • Wikipedia, "Brainfuck": the Wikipedia article. Used for orientation and for its list of references, not as evidence.

    Why read it: A fair overview with a good reference list. Check its details against the sources: see History for one date that does not match the archive.

  • Wikipedia, "Halting problem": the Wikipedia article on the halting problem. Our source for the statement of Turing's result (A. M. Turing, "On Computable Numbers, with an Application to the Entscheidungsproblem", Proceedings of the London Mathematical Society, 1936 to 1937), which we did not open.

    Why read it: Orientation on the one question no program can answer about all programs.
